Chart of accounts with AI: build yours (free template)

Your software came with 80 accounts you never asked for. Half your spending lands in “Miscellaneous,” and you’re not sure which account a new charge belongs in.

By: Computatio · Last reviewed 2026-11-09

Quick answer: A chart of accounts is the list of buckets your books sort every dollar into: assets, liabilities, equity, income and expenses. To build one with AI, give it your business type and how you get paid, and ask for a short list of accounts with a number, a type and a one-line description, with each expense account tied to a Schedule C line. Then you trim it, check it against the 2025 Schedule C, and load it into your software. Keep it short: about 20 to 35 accounts covers most one-person businesses.

Your chart of accounts is a junk drawer

Some accounts you never use. Some overlap, like “Software,” “Subscriptions” and “Dues.” And one account, “Other,” holds a third of your spending.

When the buckets are wrong, every report built on them is wrong too.

And tax time means sorting it all again

Your accounts don’t match the lines on your tax return. So each spring, you or someone you pay re-sorts a year of expenses by hand to fit Schedule C.

What a messy chart of accounts costs you

  • Some lines have their own rules. The 2025 Schedule C instructions say most business meals are only 50% deductible, and meals go on line 24b, apart from travel on line 24a. Lump them into “Travel” and the limit gets missed.
  • Some costs aren’t deductible at all. The same instructions say you can’t deduct the base rate of the first phone line into your home. A vague “Utilities” account hides that.
  • “Other” still needs detail. For 2025, other expenses are listed in Part V (line 48) and carried to line 27b. A big catch-all account means itemizing it later anyway.
  • Your records stay around. The IRS says to keep most records at least 3 years, and employment tax records at least 4. A clear chart makes old records readable when you need them.

Picture books that sort themselves into your tax return

Every expense account points to one Schedule C line. At year-end you run a profit and loss report, and it already reads like your return. Nothing is labeled “Other” without a reason.

You spend tax season checking numbers, not re-sorting them.

Owner draws and contributions are not income or expenses. They track money moving between you and the business. Home office costs usually aren’t a chart account; they’re figured separately and go on line 30.

State note. In California, the CDTFA says you need a seller’s permit (there’s no fee) if you sell or lease goods that would normally be subject to sales tax, and you must report and pay that tax. If that’s you, keep a “Sales tax payable” account so the tax you collect isn’t mixed into your sales. Other states have their own rules; check your state’s tax agency.

How to build a chart of accounts with AI: the 4-step method

This works in ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or Copilot. You don’t need to be technical, and you don’t need to share any bank data to do it.

  1. Describe your business in three lines. What you sell, how customers pay you, and your five biggest costs. No names or account numbers; see our data safety guide.
  2. Run the prompt below. It asks for a short list, each account tied to a tax line, with a reason.
  3. Trim and rename. Delete accounts you won’t use this year. Merge any two you can’t tell apart. Use the plain names you’d search for.
  4. Load it and test it. Enter the accounts in your software, then sort one month of transactions into them with our AI prompts to categorize transactions. Any charge with no clear home means a missing account or a fuzzy description.
Prompt you can copy:
“Build a chart of accounts for a sole proprietor who [what you do]. Customers pay by [methods]. Main costs: [list]. I [do / don’t] sell physical products and [do / don’t] collect sales tax. Return a table with account number, account name, type (asset, liability, equity, income, cost of goods, expense), a one-line description of what goes in it, and the 2025 Schedule C line each income and expense account rolls up to. Keep it under 35 accounts. Keep meals separate from travel. Don’t include accounts I didn’t describe a need for, and flag any line you’re unsure about instead of guessing.”
Example (made-up data). A freelance web designer runs the prompt. She’s paid by bank transfer and card through an online invoicing tool. Her main costs are software, a coworking desk, contractors and a laptop.
AI returns 24 accounts. Three come back flagged: “Laptop: expense or equipment? Depends on cost and how you handle depreciation; ask your tax preparer.” “Coworking desk: rent (20b) or office expense (18)?” “Card processing fees: confirm line 10.”
Result: She deletes four accounts she’ll never use, settles the three flags with her tax preparer, and loads 20 accounts.

How do you check an AI chart of accounts?

  1. Check every Schedule C line against the 2025 form. A line number AI got wrong will follow you all year.
  2. Look for the balance sheet. You need at least one bank account, any credit cards, and owner draws and contributions. Without them, you can’t reconcile; see AI bank reconciliation.
  3. Keep “Other” small. If one account would hold more than a few percent of your spending, give it a real name.

Limits: what AI can’t decide for you

AI doesn’t know your facts. Whether a laptop is depreciated, whether a car cost is business, or how much of your phone is business use all depend on your records. IRS Publication 583 says you can use any recordkeeping system that clearly shows your income and expenses, and notes that double-entry bookkeeping has built-in checks. A good chart is the start; reconciling every month keeps it honest.
