Bookkeeping

How to do your own bookkeeping when you’re self-employed

You know you should keep books. You just don’t know where to start, and the shoebox of receipts keeps growing.

By: Computatio · Last reviewed 2026-11-16

Quick answer. To do your own bookkeeping when you’re self-employed, keep business money in its own account, sort every transaction into the expense lines on Schedule C, save receipts, reconcile each account to its statement every month, and set aside money for tax. About 15 minutes a week and 30 minutes a month keeps it current.

You’re not sure what “doing the books” even means

Most freelancers and owner-operators were never taught bookkeeping. So it stays on the to-do list. Bank transactions pile up, and you tell yourself you’ll sort it out before taxes.

Bookkeeping is just a record of money in and money out, sorted so you can see your profit. That’s it.

Your business and personal money are tangled

The second problem is the mix. Coffee, client payments, software and groceries all run through one card. Every transaction becomes a question: was that business?

What it costs to wait until tax time

Self-employment tax alone is 15.3% (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare), and it applies once your net earnings reach $400. Income tax comes on top. If you don’t track profit during the year, that bill arrives as a surprise.

Records matter after you file too. The IRS can generally assess more tax for 3 years after you file. That window is 6 years if you leave out income worth more than 25% of the gross income on your return. Without receipts, a deduction you really earned is hard to defend.

Picture this instead

It’s the first week of the month. You open one account, see every transaction already sorted, and know your profit to the dollar. You move your tax money aside and close the laptop. In January, your full-year report lines up with your Schedule C, and filing takes an afternoon.

The one-hour bookkeeping routine for the self-employed

  1. Open a separate business account. Use one checking account, and one card if you need it, for business only. You don’t need an LLC; a second account used only for business works. If your money is already mixed, pick a start date and separate from there. See how to separate business and personal expenses with AI.
  2. Pick one place for your books. A spreadsheet works for a few dozen transactions a month. Past that, software that imports bank transactions saves time. What matters most is one system, kept current.
  3. Use Schedule C lines as your categories. Sole proprietors report on Schedule C, and so do most single-member LLCs unless they chose to be taxed as a corporation. Use its expense lines, such as car and truck expenses, contract labor, insurance, office expenses, supplies, travel, meals and utilities. Add two non-expense categories: owner draws (money you take out) and owner contributions (personal money you put in). Our chart of accounts guide has a template.
  4. Categorize every week. Fifteen minutes. Add a short note to anything unusual, because you won’t remember in April.
  5. Save receipts and documents. Phone photos of receipts, invoices, 1099s and statements, in one folder per year. If you have employees, keep employment tax records at least 4 years.
  6. Reconcile each account monthly. Compare your books’ ending balance with the bank or card statement. If they differ, look for missing, duplicate or mistyped entries. A month that reconciles is a month you can trust.
  7. Check profit and set aside tax. Income minus expenses, every month. Move a fixed share of each payment into savings for estimated taxes. See our guide to quarterly estimated taxes.
  8. Close the year in January. Reconcile December, clear any uncategorized items, and list contractors you paid. For payments made in 2026, you generally file Form 1099-NEC for a contractor you paid $2,000 or more (it was $600 for earlier years).
State note. In California, if you sell or lease taxable physical products, you generally need a free seller’s permit from the California Department of Tax and Fee Administration (CDTFA). Sales tax you collect isn’t your income, so track it separately.

Let AI do the weekly sorting

You don’t have to be technical. Export last week’s transactions as a spreadsheet and paste them into any AI chat tool with this prompt:

Prompt. “I’m a self-employed [your work]. Sort these transactions into Schedule C expense categories, plus Owner draw, Owner contribution and Income. Return a table with date, description, amount, category and a confidence of high or low. Mark anything that could be personal as low.”

Example (made-up data):

DateDescriptionAmountCategoryConfidence
Nov 3Adobe subscription-$59.99Office expensesHigh
Nov 4Client payment, Lane Studio$1,800.00IncomeHigh
Nov 5Transfer to personal checking-$1,000.00Owner drawHigh
Nov 6Costco-$142.18SuppliesLow

Check the output before you save it. Open every low-confidence line and decide yourself; the Costco run could be groceries. Make sure the table’s total matches your bank’s total for the week. Remove account numbers before you paste, and read how to keep your financial data safe when you use AI. AI sorts; you still reconcile, keep receipts and own your return.

How much time does it take?

  • Weekly (15 minutes): categorize and save receipts.
  • Monthly (30 minutes): reconcile, review profit, move tax money.
  • Quarterly: pay estimated taxes.
  • Yearly: close the books, send 1099s, file.
