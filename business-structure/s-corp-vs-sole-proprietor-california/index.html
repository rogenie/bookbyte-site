S-corp vs. sole proprietor in California: when the switch pays off

Your profit went up, and so did the self-employment tax bill. Someone told you to “just become an S-corp.” Before you do, here is what changes, what it costs in California, and how to find your own break-even.

By: Computatio · Last reviewed 2026-11-02

Quick answer: as a sole proprietor, you pay 15.3% self-employment tax on 92.35% of your net profit. As an S-corp owner, you pay Social Security and Medicare tax only on the reasonable salary you pay yourself, not on the rest of the profit. In California, an S-corp also owes 1.5% of its net income, with an $800 yearly minimum, plus payroll and an extra tax return. The switch tends to pay off only when your profit is well above a fair salary for your work.

Self-employment tax takes a big bite

As a sole proprietor (or a single-member LLC taxed the default way), you pay self-employment tax on your profit. The rate is 15.3%: 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. It applies to 92.35% of your net earnings.

The Social Security part stops at a yearly cap, $184,500 for 2026. Medicare has no cap. Below the cap, almost every extra dollar of profit carries this tax, on top of income tax.

S-corp vs. sole proprietor in California: the advice conflicts

One person says an S-corp is a must. Another says it’s a trap. Both can be right, because the answer depends on your numbers.

Here is the difference. In an S-corp, you’re an employee of your own company. You pay yourself a salary through payroll, and Social Security and Medicare taxes apply to that salary. Profit you take out beyond the salary, as distributions, isn’t subject to those taxes. That gap is the whole reason people switch.

Outside California, the federal side works the same way, but state taxes and fees differ. Check your own state’s rules.

What an S-corp costs you in California

  • California S-corp tax: 1.5% of the company’s California net income, with an $800 minimum each year, even in a loss year.
  • Payroll: you run payroll for yourself, withhold and pay payroll taxes, and file payroll reports. California requires you to register with the Employment Development Department (EDD) within 15 days of paying more than $100 in wages in a quarter. Most owners pay a payroll service.
  • An extra tax return: federal Form 1120-S and California Form 100S each year, usually prepared for a fee.
  • Clean books: an S-corp needs reconciled books and no mixing of personal and business money. See how to reconcile your bank account.
State note. California waives the $800 minimum for a newly formed S corporation’s first tax year, but first-year income is still taxed at 1.5%. California LLCs owe their own $800 annual tax starting in the first year; the temporary first-year exemption ended with tax years starting before 2024. If you plan to form an LLC and elect S-corp status, confirm with the Franchise Tax Board which rules apply to you.

The salary rule can undo the plan

The IRS says an S-corp must pay an owner who works in the business reasonable pay for that work before taking distributions. If the salary is too low, the IRS can treat distributions as wages and charge employment taxes on them.

The IRS looks at things like your training, your duties, the time you put in, and what similar businesses pay for similar work. A fair test: what would you have to pay someone else to do your job? If most of your profit comes from your own work, most of it may need to be salary, and the gap shrinks.

Picture this instead

You know your break-even number. You know the salary you’d pay yourself and why it’s defensible. You can see, line by line, what the S-corp would cost in fees, tax and time. You decide once, with your own figures, and stop second-guessing every time someone mentions it.

When does an S-corp pay off? The break-even method

You can do this with a calculator or any AI chat tool. You don’t need to be technical. Use your real numbers from your books, not a guess.

  1. Get your net profit. Use last year’s Schedule C profit or a 12-month total from your books. If your books are behind, catch them up first; see how to use AI for bookkeeping.
  2. Pick a reasonable salary. Write down what it would cost to hire someone to do your work, and why.
  3. Get quotes for a payroll service and for the extra business return.
  4. Run the comparison with the prompt below.
  5. Check the math yourself with the formulas in the example.
Prompt to copy: “Compare two setups for a California business. Net profit: [amount]. Setup A, sole proprietor: self-employment tax = 15.3% × 92.35% × net profit. Setup B, S-corp: payroll tax = 15.3% × salary of [amount]; California S-corp tax = the greater of $800 or 1.5% of (net profit − salary − 7.65% of salary); plus payroll service [amount] and tax return fee [amount]. Show each line with its formula, then the difference. Don’t estimate income tax or add any other taxes. List anything you assumed.”

The example also skips income tax effects, such as the deduction for half of self-employment tax and the qualified business income deduction, which can change the result.

How to check the AI’s answer

  • Redo each line with a calculator using the formulas above. AI tools still slip on arithmetic.
  • Make sure it used your salary, not a lower number it picked.
  • Delete any tax it added that you didn’t ask for, and any it left out that applies to you.

Limits: this is a first screen, not a tax plan. It doesn’t cover income tax, retirement contributions, health insurance or other states. If profit is modest or swings a lot, the fixed costs usually eat the gap. Keep paying quarterly estimated taxes on time either way; more in our estimated tax guides.

How to switch to an S-corp in California

  1. Form a corporation or LLC with the California Secretary of State, if you haven’t already.
  2. File IRS Form 2553 to elect S-corp status. To count for a tax year, file it no more than 2 months and 15 days after that year begins (or any time in the year before). The IRS has relief for late elections in some cases.
  3. Register with the EDD as an employer and set up payroll.
  4. Open a business bank account and keep the books separate from day one. Our bookkeeping routine for the self-employed shows how.
